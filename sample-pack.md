# Claim Zero — Sample Discovery Pack

This sample shows the format of the €29 founding pilot. It is not a paid deliverable and makes no claim that these subjects are globally unknown. The point is evidence quality, uncertainty handling, and editorial usefulness.

## Lead 1 — The World War II prison break that became a mass death event

**Hook**  
At about 2 a.m. on 5 August 1944, Japanese prisoners at Cowra, Australia, charged barbed wire and machine-gun positions with improvised weapons after a bugle signal. Hundreds escaped the compound, but the breakout became one of the bloodiest prison escapes of the war. Australian official sources record more than 200 Japanese deaths and four Australian deaths across the breakout and recapture period.

**Why it works editorially**  
The immediate image is unusually strong: a secret vote, an unauthorised bugle, improvised weapons, burning huts and a breakout that many participants could not realistically expect to survive. The deeper story is not simply escape; it is the collision between wartime captivity, military culture, shame, duty and the Geneva Convention treatment of prisoners.

**Suggested angle**  
`Why would hundreds of prisoners attempt an escape they were unlikely to survive?`

**Evidence / sources**  
- Australian War Memorial — Cowra breakout: https://www.awm.gov.au/commemoration/cowra-breakout
- National Archives of Australia — Cowra breakout, 1944: https://www.naa.gov.au/help-your-research/fact-sheets/cowra-breakout-1944
- Australian War Memorial — The bugle and the breakout: https://www.awm.gov.au/articles/blog/the-bugle-and-the-breakout

**Uncertainty / caution**  
Official sources differ slightly on the Japanese death count (231 versus 234 depending on scope/accounting). A responsible script should state the source used rather than flattening the discrepancy.

## Lead 2 — Britain preserved a dead rat as part of the national archival record

**Hook**  
In 1836, civil servant Henry Cole gave evidence about the appalling condition of Britain’s public records and physically produced vermin remains before a Parliamentary committee. The National Archives still keeps two rat remains associated with that episode; one is informally known as “Sir Henry Cole’s rat.” The scandal over neglected records contributed to reforms culminating in the Public Record Office Act 1838.

**Why it works editorially**  
It is a near-perfect short-form object story: one grotesque physical artifact opens into the history of how a modern national archive was created. Cole later became the first director of what became the Victoria and Albert Museum and commissioned the first commercial Christmas card, giving the story an additional character twist.

**Suggested angle**  
`The dead rat that helped change how Britain preserves history.`

**Evidence / sources**  
- The National Archives — Sir Henry Cole’s rat: https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/stories/sir-henry-coles-rat/
- The National Archives — Forty Years at Kew speech, noting Cole brandishing a rat in Parliament and the 1838 establishment of the Public Record Office: https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/speeches/jeff-james-forty-years-at-kew-2018-02-21.pdf
- Contemporary material reproduced in *Fifty Years of Public Work of Sir Henry Cole* records parliamentary discussion of rat remains among the records.

**Uncertainty / caution**  
Popular retellings simplify the chain of causation. The rat did not single-handedly create the Public Record Office; it was vivid evidence within a broader reform campaign. The National Archives’ own detailed account is the safest wording guide.

## Lead 3 — The alleged plan to rescue Napoleon with primitive submarines

**Hook**  
During Napoleon’s exile on St Helena, British smuggler and adventurer Tom Johnson claimed he had been offered £40,000 to help rescue the former emperor using primitive submarines and a cliff-lowering system. The story sounds invented — and parts may have been embellished — but archival evidence supports the existence of Johnson’s submarine work, while contemporary and later accounts document submarine rescue proposals around St Helena.

**Why it works editorially**  
The tension is built into the sourcing: the audience begins with an apparently absurd claim, then discovers that the machinery and official interest were more real than expected, while the exact rescue plot remains uncertain. It is a good example of how to tell a bizarre historical story without pretending uncertainty has disappeared.

**Suggested angle**  
`The Napoleon submarine rescue plot: brilliant plan, self-promotion, or both?`

**Evidence / sources**  
- Smithsonian Magazine / historian Mike Dash — The Secret Plot to Rescue Napoleon by Submarine: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-secret-plot-to-rescue-napoleon-by-submarine-1194764/
- U.S. Naval Institute Proceedings (1943) — They Wanted to Rescue Napoleon: https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1943/june/they-wanted-rescue-napoleon
- Public Domain Review — *Napoleon at St Helena* and discussion of reported rescue plots: https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/napoleon-at-st-helena-1855

**Uncertainty / caution**  
Do not script the rescue attempt as an established executed operation. Johnson was a self-promoter, reliable records of his life are incomplete, and Smithsonian’s analysis explicitly distinguishes evidence for the submarine from proof that the full rescue scenario was operationally attempted.

## Pilot quality rule

A paid pack should be better than this sample in one important respect: it is tailored to the buyer’s explicit brief and acceptance criteria before payment. If the agreed criteria cannot be met, the work should not be represented as a successful delivery.
